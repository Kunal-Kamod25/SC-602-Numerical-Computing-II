2. Estimate f(1):

3. Estimate f(2):
f(2) = 2.4667
4. Why is Lagrange interpolation particularly convenient here?
The x-values in this data are not evenly spaced — the gaps are 0.5, then 1.0, then 1.5. Lagrange interpolation doesn't care about equal spacing at all; the formula just uses whatever x-values you give it directly. Other methods, like Newton's forward or backward difference formulas, are built around a constant step size h between points, so they can't be applied directly to unevenly spaced data like this without modification. That's exactly the situation here, which is why Lagrange is the more convenient choice.

A student claims: "Since four data points are given, the interpolating polynomial must always be a cubic polynomial."
1. Is the statement mathematically correct?
No. With 4 data points, the Lagrange formula can produce a polynomial of degree at most n − 1 = 3, but "at most" does not mean it will always be exactly degree 3 — that depends entirely on the data.
2. Lagrange interpolating polynomial:
P(x) = x²   (all higher-order terms cancelled out to 0 after simplifying)
3. Degree of the resulting polynomial:
Degree 2, not degree 3.
4. Why can the polynomial have degree less than n − 1 even with n points given?
Looking at the data, f(x) = x² fits every single point exactly: (1,1), (2,4), (3,9), (4,16). Since one quadratic already passes through all four points perfectly, when you actually expand the Lagrange formula the coefficients on the x³ term all cancel out to zero. So even though the formula is set up to allow up to degree 3, the "extra" cubic part isn't needed and disappears. In general, the degree from n points is at most n − 1, but it comes out lower whenever the given data happens to already lie exactly on a lower-degree curve.
