ANSWERS
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1. Does Richardson always give a smaller error than Central Difference?
    In the regime where truncation error dominates (roughly h = 1e-1 down to
    about h = 1e-5), yes: R(h) is consistently more accurate than D(h) for every
    function tested, often by several orders of magnitude, because Richardson
    cancels the leading O(h^2) error term of the central difference formula.
    However, once h becomes extremely small (see Q3/Q6), floating-point
    round-off error dominates and Richardson's advantage shrinks or can even
    reverse, since R(h) combines two nearly-equal, noisy D(h) values and can
    amplify that round-off noise.

Q2. By approximately what factor does the error decrease when h is reduced by 10?
    Central Difference is O(h^2), so reducing h by a factor of 10 should reduce
    its error by a factor of about 10^2 = 100.
    Richardson Extrapolation is O(h^4), so reducing h by a factor of 10 should
    reduce its error by a factor of about 10^4 = 10000.
    This matches the observed slopes in the table above (while truncation error
    dominates): slope ~ -2 for D(h) and slope ~ -4 for R(h) on the log-log plot.

Q3. Does Richardson continue to improve as h becomes very small?
    No. Richardson's error decreases rapidly at first (as O(h^4) predicts), but
    for very small h (typically below ~1e-4 to 1e-5 depending on the function)
    the error stops decreasing and eventually starts increasing again. This is
    because subtracting nearly-equal floating point numbers f(x+h) and f(x-h)
    loses significant digits (catastrophic cancellation), and Richardson's extra
    combination step (4*D(h/2) - D(h)) further amplifies this round-off noise.

Q4. Does the log-log slope support the theoretical prediction?
    Yes, over the range where truncation error dominates. The 'truncation-region'
    slopes in the table above are close to 2 for Central Difference and close to
    3-4 for Richardson Extrapolation, matching the theoretical orders O(h^2) and
    O(h^4). The separate 'full-range' slope (fit across every h value, including
    the ones where round-off already dominates) is much flatter/noisier, because
    it mixes a decreasing-error region with an increasing-error region -- this is
    itself evidence of the round-off floor discussed in Q6, not a contradiction
    of the theory.

Q5. Does Richardson achieve the expected O(h^4) behavior?
    Yes, in the intermediate h range. As h decreases from 1e-1 toward about 1e-4,
    R(h)'s error drops far faster than D(h)'s error, consistent with O(h^4) vs
    O(h^2) convergence. This is confirmed both by the log-log plots (steeper
    slope for R(h)) and by the near -4 observed slope values.

Q6. What role does floating-point round-off error play?
    Every function evaluation in double precision carries a relative round-off
    error of about 1e-16. The central difference formula divides a small
    difference f(x+h)-f(x-h) by 2h; as h shrinks, that difference shrinks toward
    the size of the round-off noise itself, so the *relative* round-off error in
    the result grows like O(machine_epsilon / h). Total error is therefore the
    sum of a decreasing truncation term (O(h^2) or O(h^4)) and this growing
    round-off term. There is an optimal h that minimizes total error; pushing h
    smaller than that (roughly h < 1e-5 to 1e-6 for Central Difference, and even
    larger for Richardson, since it involves an extra subtraction) makes results
    worse, not better. This is exactly the up-turn visible at the right-hand side
    of the log-log error plots and in the results table for h = 1e-7 .. 1e-12.
